# CHVE-2026-0001 – Authority Bias

## Summary

**Authority Bias** is a recurring human cognitive vulnerability in which individuals disproportionately trust, comply with, or defer to perceived authority figures or signals of authority, even when such deference conflicts with evidence, logic, or their own interests.

This vulnerability is widely exploitable in social, economic, political, and technical contexts.

---

## Description

Humans routinely rely on authority as a cognitive shortcut. Titles, uniforms, credentials, institutional affiliation, seniority, or confident assertion can substitute for independent evaluation. Laboratory research has long shown elevated compliance under perceived authority (Milgram, 1963); contemporary fraud and social-engineering cases show the same heuristic exploited outside experimental settings.

While this heuristic is often adaptive, it becomes a vulnerability when authority cues are:

* fabricated
* exaggerated
* irrelevant to the decision at hand
* used to suppress scrutiny or dissent

Authority Bias does not require obedience, belief, or agreement. Partial compliance, delayed resistance, or reduced scrutiny is often sufficient for exploitation.

---

## Affected Population

* Humans

This vulnerability manifests at the individual cognitive level but is observable across populations.

---

## Preconditions

Authority Bias is more likely to manifest when one or more of the following conditions are present:

* Information asymmetry
* Time pressure or urgency
* Stress, fear, or perceived risk
* Hierarchical or institutional contexts
* Cultural norms emphasizing respect for authority
* Lack of domain expertise by the target

---

## Attack Vectors

Common vectors include:

* Impersonation of officials, experts, or institutions
* Use of uniforms, branding, seals, or formal language
* Invocation of credentials, titles, or seniority
* Appeals to policy, regulation, or unnamed oversight bodies
* Framing dissent as noncompliance or incompetence

---

## Exploit Examples

Illustrative patterns (not exhaustive):

* Scam calls impersonating tax authorities, police, or banks
* Fraudulent investment advice citing fabricated credentials
* Workplace manipulation leveraging job titles or hierarchy
* Social engineering attacks invoking IT or security authority
* Pseudoscientific claims relying on prestige rather than evidence

---

## Documented Real-World Exploitation

Public enforcement data and landmark cases show Authority Bias being exploited at scale through impersonation of governments, businesses, and organizational roles:

* **Government and business imposter scams (population-scale):** In 2025, people reported losing about **$3.5 billion** to imposter scams in the United States—the most reported fraud category that year (nearly one in three fraud reports). Reported losses included nearly **$1 billion** to business impersonators (with bank impersonators among the costliest) and about **$920 million** to government impersonators (Federal Trade Commission [FTC], 2026).
* **Business Email Compromise / executive and vendor authority (organizational-scale):** The FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3) recorded **24,768** Business Email Compromise (BEC) complaints with about **$3.05 billion** in reported losses in 2025 (Federal Bureau of Investigation [FBI], 2026). BEC commonly abuses perceived authority of executives, finance staff, or trusted vendors to authorize payments without adequate verification.
* **Landmark case — vendor impersonation against major firms:** Evaldas Rimasauskas was sentenced to five years in prison for a BEC scheme that tricked employees of major U.S. technology companies into wiring more than **$120 million** to accounts he controlled by impersonating a legitimate business counterparty (U.S. Department of Justice / FBI reporting, 2019–2020).

These cases illustrate probabilistic, high-volume exploitation: attackers need reliable deference to authority cues, not universal compliance.

---

## Impact

Potential impacts include:

* Financial loss
* Loss of autonomy or consent
* Acceptance of false information
* Suppression of critical thinking
* Escalation of further exploitation

Impact severity varies by context and degree of compliance.

---

## Exploit Reliability

* **Type:** Probabilistic
* **Characteristics:**

  * Non-universal
  * Context-dependent
  * Scales with exposure volume

Authority Bias remains exploitable despite widespread awareness.

---

## Population Convergence

While individuals differ in skepticism and experience, Authority Bias remains observable across:

* Cultures
* Age groups
* Education levels
* Socioeconomic contexts

Aggregate exploit success scales with exposure, independent of individual learning effects.

---

## Mitigations (“Human Patches”)

Mitigations may reduce but not eliminate risk:

* Explicit verification procedures
* Separation of authority from decision approval
* Slowing high-stakes decisions
* Encouraging challenge and dissent
* Training focused on authority impersonation tactics

Mitigations are context-dependent and unevenly adopted.

## Notes

Authority Bias is not a moral failing or a sign of weakness. It is a common heuristic that becomes exploitable under specific conditions.

Documenting this vulnerability does not imply inevitability or universality.
